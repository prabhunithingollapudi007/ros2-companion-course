So far ROS has been bodiless: nodes passing messages. To draw a robot, simulate it, or say where its camera is pointing, ROS needs a description of its body: the rigid parts, and the joints that connect them and let them move. That description is **URDF**, an XML file, and **xacro** is the macro language that keeps it short. A node called **robot_state_publisher** combines the description with the current joint angles and works out where every part is, which is what RViz draws and what the frames of Lesson 8 are made of.

## Links and joints

<LinkAndJoint />

The lesson's example is a small rover: a box for a body, two driven wheels, a ball caster at the back to rest on, and a camera on top that can pan. Its links and joints form this tree:

<RoverTree />

Each link has its own coordinate frame. A link says nothing about where it is; its frame is placed by the joint above it. Here is the body, with the numbers it uses declared at the top of the file:

<Listing {...code.rover} lines={roverPart('<!-- Dimensions', '</link>')} highlight={at(code.rover, '<link name="base_link">', '<visual>', '<collision>', '<inertial>')} />

The `${...}` parts are xacro, which comes below; for now read them as the numbers they name. A box's geometry is centred on the link's frame, so this body reaches 0.20 m forward and back, 0.15 m to each side, and 0.05 m up and down.

<Derivation title="The inertia of a box and a cylinder" why="Every link that a simulator moves needs a mass and an inertia, and getting them badly wrong makes simulated robots jitter, tip or fly off. These two formulas cover most simple parts.">

For a solid box of mass $m$ and sides $x$, $y$, $z$, centred on its frame, the moments of inertia about its three axes are

$$
I_{xx} = \frac{m}{12}\,(y^2 + z^2), \qquad I_{yy} = \frac{m}{12}\,(x^2 + z^2), \qquad I_{zz} = \frac{m}{12}\,(x^2 + y^2).
$$

Each one adds up $\int r^2\,dm$ over the box, where $r$ is the distance from the axis; turning about $x$, only the $y$ and $z$ extents are in the way. The off-diagonal terms are zero because the box is symmetric about its frame.

For a solid cylinder of radius $r$ and length $h$ along its $z$ axis,

$$
I_{zz} = \frac{m r^2}{2}, \qquad I_{xx} = I_{yy} = \frac{m}{12}\,(3r^2 + h^2).
$$

For the rover's body, $m = 4$ kg and the box is 0.40 × 0.30 × 0.10 m, so $I_{xx} = \tfrac{4}{12}(0.09 + 0.01) \approx 0.0333$ kg·m². That is the number xacro wrote, further down.

</Derivation>

## Joints: where the child is, and how it moves

The camera's joint shows every part of a joint at once:

<Listing {...code.rover} lines={roverPart('<!-- A ball at the back', '</robot>')} highlight={at(code.rover, '<joint name="camera_pan" type="revolute">', '<origin xyz="${base_length/2 - 0.05}', '<axis xyz="0 0 1"/>', '<limit lower')} />

- **origin** puts the child's frame in the parent's: the camera sits 0.15 m forward of the body's centre and 0.07 m up. It can also turn the child's frame, with `rpy`: roll, pitch and yaw in radians, about the parent's x, y and z axes in that order.
- **axis** is what the joint turns about, in the child's frame. `0 0 1` is the camera's z axis, pointing up, so it pans left and right.
- **limit** bounds a revolute or prismatic joint: here ±π/2, a quarter of a turn either way. The caster's joint is fixed, so it has neither axis nor limit.

The wheels need one more idea. A URDF cylinder is drawn along its own z axis, and the wheels' should point sideways. So each wheel joint turns the wheel's frame with `rpy="-π/2 0 0"`, a roll of −90° that tips the z axis from pointing up to pointing left, along the robot's y axis. The wheel then spins about its own z axis, `axis xyz="0 0 1"`, which is the robot's y. Both wheels get the same turn, so a positive angle rolls either of them forward.

## Xacro: properties, maths and macros

Plain URDF has no variables, so a robot with two identical wheels repeats the same 20 lines twice with different numbers, and changing the wheel size means changing it everywhere. **Xacro** adds what was missing, then writes plain URDF:

- `<xacro:property name="wheel_radius" value="0.08"/>` names a value; `${wheel_radius}` uses it.
- `${...}` is a Python expression, so `${base_width/2 + wheel_width/2}` and `${-pi/2}` work.
- `<xacro:macro name="wheel" params="side reflect">` defines a reusable block; `<xacro:wheel side="left" reflect="1"/>` stamps out a copy with those parameters filled in.

<Listing {...code.rover} lines={roverPart('<!-- One macro', '<xacro:wheel side="right"')} highlight={at(code.rover, '<xacro:macro name="wheel"', '<link name="${side}_wheel">', '<joint name="${side}_wheel_joint"', '<origin xyz="${wheel_x}', '<xacro:wheel side="left"', '<xacro:wheel side="right"')} />

<PlainWords>

xacro evaluates `${...}` as Python and writes the result with Python's number formatting. In binary floating point, 0.15 + 0.02 is not quite 0.17, so the wheel lands at y = 0.16999999999999998 m. That is about 2 × 10⁻¹⁷ m off, far below anything physical, and every program reading the URDF parses it back to the same number. It looks odd, and is fine.

</PlainWords>

## Showing the robot

<Listing {...code.viewLaunch} highlight={at(code.viewLaunch, "robot_description = ParameterValue(Command(['xacro ', model]), value_type=str)", "parameters=[{'robot_description': robot_description}]", "executable='joint_state_publisher_gui'", "executable='joint_state_publisher',", "package='rviz2'")} />

Three nodes, each with one job:

- **robot_state_publisher** gets the robot's URDF in its parameter `robot_description`. `Command(['xacro ', model])` runs xacro when the launch starts, so the parameter holds the expanded URDF; robot_state_publisher never sees xacro. `ParameterValue(..., value_type=str)` tells launch to pass the text as it is rather than try to read it as YAML. The node also republishes the description on the topic `/robot_description`, which is where RViz and joint_state_publisher read it.
- **joint_state_publisher** publishes `/joint_states`, a position for each movable joint, all zero; **joint_state_publisher_gui** does the same with a slider for each. On a real robot, the motor drivers publish `/joint_states` instead.
- **rviz2** draws it, with a layout from `view.rviz` that shows the model and its frames.

The package installs the model, the launch file and the layout, so `ros2 launch` can find them:

<Listing {...code.cmake} highlight={at(code.cmake, 'install(DIRECTORY')} />

Launched with `gui:=false`, so the plain joint_state_publisher runs:

robot_state_publisher's warning is harmless here. It computes positions with a library called KDL, which has no use for the mass of the root link and says so. Many robots add a massless root link called `base_footprint`, on the ground under the robot, which also silences it.

The fourth node is RViz's own: it listens to the transforms on `/tf` and `/tf_static`. Only the three movable joints are in `/joint_states`; the caster's fixed joint has nothing to report, so robot_state_publisher publishes it once, on `/tf_static`.

<Recap>

- A **URDF** describes a robot as **links** (rigid parts: visual, collision, inertial) connected by **joints** (fixed, revolute, continuous, prismatic) into a tree with one root.
- A joint's **origin** places the child's frame in the parent's, with `xyz` and `rpy`; its **axis** is in the child's frame; **limits** bound revolute and prismatic joints.
- **Xacro** adds properties, `${...}` Python expressions and macros, then writes plain URDF. `xacro file.urdf.xacro` shows the result; `check_urdf` prints the tree.
- **robot_state_publisher** takes the URDF as its `robot_description` parameter, listens to `/joint_states`, and publishes where every link is: `/tf` for moving joints, `/tf_static` for fixed ones.
- **joint_state_publisher(_gui)** stands in for the motors' joint states; RViz draws the model from `/robot_description` and the frames.
- Next: those frames, and how to ask where anything is relative to anything else, at any moment.

</Recap>
