## Frames and transforms

A **frame** is an origin and three axes: x forward, y to the left, z up, by ROS's convention. A **transform** from a parent frame to a child frame is the child's pose in the parent: a translation, where the child's origin is, and a rotation, which way its axes point. The same numbers convert coordinates: a point $p_c$ given in the child frame is, in the parent frame,

$$
p_{\text{parent}} = R\,p_{c} + t ,
$$

where $R$ is the rotation and $t$ the translation. Transforms chain: to get from the camera to the map, apply camera → base_link, then base_link → odom, then odom → map.

`static_transform_publisher` publishes one transform that never changes. Here it says that `odom` sits 0.5 m along x and 0.2 m back along y on the map, turned 0.3 rad (17°) to the left:

<Terminal run={[r['static-pub'], r['tf-static-echo']]} />

A transform travels as a `geometry_msgs/msg/TransformStamped`: a header with the parent frame and a stamp, the child frame's name, a translation, and a rotation as a **quaternion**, four numbers instead of three angles. `tf2_echo` asks tf2 for a transform and prints it in every form:

<Terminal run={r['tf2-echo-static']} />

`At time 0.0` is how tf2 marks static transforms: valid at every time. Asked the other way round, tf2 inverts the transform. Note that the translation is not simply negated: going back means turning back first.

<Terminal run={r['tf2-echo-inverse']} />

<QuaternionPlayground />

<PlainWords>

A quaternion for a turn by angle θ about an axis is the axis times sin(θ/2), followed by cos(θ/2). For a robot on the floor the axis is z, so only z = sin(θ/2) and w = cos(θ/2) are non-zero: 0.149 and 0.989 for 0.3 rad. Quaternions have no trouble at pitch ±90°, where roll and yaw become the same turn and three angles stop being unique; try it above. You rarely write them by hand: `tf2::Quaternion::setRPY` makes one from angles.

</PlainWords>

## The tree, and who publishes it

<FrameTree />

Every frame has one parent, so the frames form a tree, and between any two of them there is exactly one chain. The top three names are a convention from REP 105 that most ROS software expects:

- **map**: fixed to the world. Positions in it do not drift, but they can jump when a localisation system corrects its estimate.
- **odom**: where odometry started counting. The robot's pose in it changes smoothly, without jumps, but drifts over time as wheel slip adds up.
- **base_link**: fixed to the robot, at its body.

<TryThis>

- **Move time on.** Only `base_link` and `camera` move on the map, and the object's coordinates in them change all the time, while its map coordinates stay put.
- **Turn odom.** The whole circle swings round `map`: everything below `odom` in the tree comes with it. This is what a localisation correction does.
- **Compare camera and base_link.** The camera is 0.15 m in front of `base_link`, so their coordinates of the object always differ by about 0.15 m in x.

</TryThis>

## Asking tf2

A node that wants transforms keeps a **buffer** and a **listener**. The listener subscribes to `/tf` and `/tf_static` and stores everything in the buffer; the node then asks the buffer:

<Listing {...code.whereIs} highlight={at(code.whereIs, 'buffer_ = std::make_unique<tf2_ros::Buffer>(get_clock());', 'listener_ = std::make_shared<tf2_ros::TransformListener>(*buffer_);', 'buffer_->lookupTransform("map", "camera", tf2::TimePointZero);', 'const tf2::TimePoint then', 'buffer_->lookupTransform("map", "camera", then);', 'catch (const tf2::TransformException & e)')} />

`lookupTransform(target, source, time)` returns the source frame's pose in the target frame, here the camera in the map. `TimePointZero` asks for the latest transform there is; a time point asks about that moment. Half a second apart, the camera has turned 0.25 rad and moved about 25 cm along its circle:

<Terminal run={[r['where-is'], r['tf2-echo-moving']]} />

<BufferTimeline />

The buffer interpolates between samples, but it does not guess. Ask about a moment after the newest sample and the lookup fails; ask about a frame nobody publishes and it fails too:

<Terminal run={[r['where-is-ahead'], r['tf2-echo-missing']]} />

Lookups throw `tf2::TransformException` when they cannot be answered, so wrap them in `try`, as `where_is` does. Passing a timeout, `lookupTransform(target, source, time, tf2::durationFromSec(0.1))`, makes the buffer wait that long for the data to arrive before giving up.

## Data has a time, and so does its frame

Sensor data comes stamped with the moment it was measured, in the frame of the sensor (Lesson 6). To use it elsewhere, transform it with the frames **as they were at that stamp**, not as they are when the data arrives. For a stamped message, `buffer->transform(msg, "map", timeout)` from `tf2_geometry_msgs` does this: it looks the transform up at the message's own stamp and returns the message in the target frame, with the same stamp.

<TimeTravelPlayground />

<PlainWords>

The camera says "the object is 1 m in front of me". In front of where the camera *was* when it saw it, not where the camera is now. A robot turning at half a radian per second has turned 9° in 0.3 s, and 9° at 2 m is 30 cm.

</PlainWords>

<Recap>

- A **transform** from parent to child is the child's pose in the parent, a translation and a rotation (a quaternion); it also converts the child's coordinates into the parent's.
- Frames form a **tree**: one parent each. **map → odom → base_link** is the usual top, and robot_state_publisher adds the robot's links below base_link.
- Static transforms go once on **/tf_static**; moving ones are broadcast repeatedly on **/tf**, each stamped.
- A **buffer** with a **listener** stores the last 10 s of transforms; `lookupTransform(target, source, time)` chains and interpolates, and throws when it cannot answer.
- Transform data **at its own stamp**: `buffer->transform(msg, frame, timeout)` does that for stamped messages.
- Tools: `tf2_echo`, `view_frames`, and RViz with a fixed frame.
- Next: a simulated world for the robot to drive in.

</Recap>
